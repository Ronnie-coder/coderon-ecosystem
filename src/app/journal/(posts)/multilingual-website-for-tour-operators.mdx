If you run tours, transfers or a guesthouse in South Africa, many of your guests start planning from another country. Plenty of them read English well. Many feel more confident booking in their own language.

## When it is worth it

A multilingual website makes sense when:

- A good share of your guests come from non-English-speaking countries
- You get enquiries from tour agents abroad
- Your competitors only offer English

If almost all your guests are local, spend the money on photos and a better booking form instead.

## How we did it for Gifted Tours

[Gifted Tours](/playroom/gifted-tours/) in Cape Town now has a language switcher with nine languages: English, Afrikaans, German, French, Dutch, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and Chinese. The switcher sits in the top menu with flags, so guests find it straight away.

We paired it with a booking request form that asks the questions a tour company needs answered before quoting: which tour, which vehicle, how long (half day, full day, several days, or a custom duration) and the preferred date. Guests can ask for exactly what they want, in their language.

## Doing translations well

- **Start with your key pages.** Home, tours, prices and booking matter most.
- **Have a person check the important words.** Automatic translation is a good start; a quick check by a native speaker avoids embarrassing mistakes.
- **Keep prices and contact details clear in every language.**

## Show them where they are going

Guests love to see the places before they book. On the Gifted Tours site, a large full-width gallery shows each adventure with its photo, its name and where it happens, like a sunset hike on Lion's Head, so a visitor can picture the day.
